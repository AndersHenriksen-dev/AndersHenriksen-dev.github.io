I read on the bus to and from work. Most days, that gives me one or two new facts. It does not sound like much, but if I learn one or two facts about Claude a day, I am an expert on it within a few months.

<!--more-->

In [my last post](/blogs/seemingly-useless-dtu-course/) I wrote about build-measure-learn, the cycle I use on everything I build. This post is about the idea underneath it, applied to myself instead of a product.

## A method and a philosophy

Build-measure-learn is a method. It has steps, and I use it on products: build a small version, see how people use it, decide what to change.

Continuous improvement is a philosophy. It has no fixed steps. It just means being a little better than yesterday, and I use it on myself. The Japanese word for it is kaizen. I only connected it to how I live while writing this post.

## Why I love movies about it

I keep watching movies about getting better, and I think it is because I recognize what the characters do:

- **Moneyball:** a baseball team with little money uses statistics to find out what really wins games, then changes how it picks players.
- **Groundhog Day** and **Edge of Tomorrow:** someone lives the same day over and over, and uses each repeat to get a little further.
- **Ford v Ferrari:** a team races, finds what broke, fixes it and races again, until the car can win at Le Mans.

What I relate to is the analysis. Look at what goes wrong, work out why, fix it and try again. I relate less to the films about a physical skill, such as Free Solo, even though I like them.

**Whiplash** is the warning. I sometimes cut away important parts of my life to focus, like the drummer in that film, and that is not healthy.

## Learning how to learn

I am not sure where this started. My best guess is home. My parents did not go to university, so to do well in my studies I had to work out how to learn on my own.

## What I read on the bus

I follow Finimize daily, along with Tech Brew, CFO Brew and The Daily Upside. I watch YouTube on tech and finance. And I read blogs and docs on whatever I feel I do not know well enough yet.

The amounts are small, but they add up. A fact or two each working day comes to a few hundred in a year.

## It has paid off twice

![A timeline. In 2024, my master's was done, and I spent 6 months learning finance from a Yale course, the Atrioc YouTube channel and newsletters. In 2025, that led to my first job, at Danske Bank. In 2026, at Capital Four, I read the Claude docs end to end and then built Saga](/images/blogs/learning-ahead.svg)

**Finance, before my first job.** After my master's, it took 6 months to land my first job. I saw how many people applied for every tech job, so I looked at another industry, and finance interested me. I spent those months learning it: Robert Shiller's Financial Markets course from Yale on Coursera, the Atrioc YouTube channel and finance newsletters. That helped me get my first job, as a Data Scientist at Danske Bank.

**Claude, before we used it.** At Capital Four, I knew we would move from GitHub Copilot to Claude. So I read the Claude docs from end to end before we did. When the move came, I was ready to build Saga, our team's AI collaborator and automation engine.

Both taught me the same thing. However much I know, I still have to keep learning to make it in tech.

## The same idea, applied to a project

Build-measure-learn is continuous improvement with a structure, aimed at a product. Saga and my SAS to Python agent both started as the smallest version that worked, and both kept getting better after that. I describe both in [the post on build-measure-learn](/blogs/seemingly-useless-dtu-course/#no-finish-line-saga).

## The same idea, applied to my health

I have type 1 diabetes. For a long time, I did not take it very seriously. Since then, I have improved how I manage it one step at a time.

Today I use an Omnipod DASH insulin pump, a Dexcom G7 sensor and AAPS, an open-source app that I set up myself. AAPS reads the sensor and adjusts the insulin the pump gives. That loop has replaced most of the manual steps, and I am in my target range most of the time without having to think about it.

It also connects to my work. Before I graduated, I worked at Novo Nordisk on a Python simulation of people with type 1 diabetes, and made it 60 times faster.

## Try it

Pick one thing you will need in a year that you do not know well enough today. Read about it on your way to work. A fact or two a day is enough.
